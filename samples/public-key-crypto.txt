Okay, let's get started. Last week we looked at symmetric encryption, where both sides share the same secret key. The big problem we ended with is key distribution: how do two people who have never met agree on a key over a network that an attacker can read?

So today is about public-key cryptography. Public-key cryptography is a scheme where every party has a key pair: a public key that anyone may know and a private key that never leaves its owner. Anything encrypted with the public key can only be decrypted with the matching private key. That already solves a big part of the distribution problem, because the public key can be sent in the clear.

Let's start with the historical step. The Diffie-Hellman key exchange is a protocol that lets two parties derive a shared secret over a public channel. Both sides pick a private exponent, send g to the power of that exponent modulo p, and each side raises what it receives to its own exponent. They end up with the same value, and an eavesdropper who only sees the exchanged values cannot compute it.

Why can't the eavesdropper compute it? The discrete logarithm problem is the task of finding the exponent x given g, p and g to the x modulo p. For well-chosen groups we believe this is computationally infeasible, and the security of Diffie-Hellman rests on that assumption. Note that plain Diffie-Hellman gives you no authentication at all. A man-in-the-middle attack is when an attacker sits between the two parties and runs a separate key exchange with each of them. Both victims think they talk to each other, but they actually share keys with the attacker.

Now RSA. RSA is a public-key system whose security relies on the difficulty of factoring the product of two large primes. You pick two primes p and q, compute n equals p times q, and the public key is the pair n and e. The private exponent d is the inverse of e modulo phi of n, which you can only compute easily if you know the factors. In practice e is almost always 65537, because it makes encryption fast and has no known weaknesses.

One important warning: textbook RSA is deterministic. Encrypting the same message twice gives the same ciphertext, which leaks information. Padding is the randomised encoding applied to a message before RSA encryption, and today we use OAEP for encryption. Never use RSA without padding.

Let's talk about signatures, because that's the second big use. A digital signature is a value computed with the private key that anyone can verify with the public key. It gives you integrity and authenticity, and also non-repudiation: the signer cannot later deny having signed. In practice you never sign the whole document. You hash it first and sign the hash, which is faster and avoids structural attacks.

Key sizes come up in the exam every year, so write this down. For RSA, 2048 bits is the minimum today and 3072 bits matches 128-bit security. Elliptic curve cryptography is public-key cryptography based on the algebraic structure of elliptic curves over finite fields. A 256-bit elliptic curve key gives roughly the same security as a 3072-bit RSA key, which is why phones and smart cards prefer it. Curve25519 is the curve you will see most often in modern protocols like Signal and TLS 1.3.

Remember the man-in-the-middle problem? Public keys only help if you know whose key you have. A certificate is a signed statement that binds a public key to an identity. The certificate authority is the trusted third party that checks the identity and signs the certificate. Your browser ships with a list of root certificates, and it accepts a website's key if there is a chain of signatures from the site's certificate up to one of those roots. That's the public key infrastructure, or PKI.

Certificates sometimes have to be withdrawn before they expire, for example when a private key is stolen. Revocation is the process of declaring a certificate invalid before its expiry date. The classic mechanism is the certificate revocation list, and OCSP is the online protocol where the browser asks the authority whether a single certificate is still valid.

Finally, a quick look ahead. Forward secrecy is the property that a stolen long-term key does not reveal past session keys. TLS 1.3 gets it by doing an ephemeral Diffie-Hellman exchange for every connection and only using the long-term key to sign. And the elephant in the room: Shor's algorithm is a quantum algorithm that factors integers and computes discrete logarithms in polynomial time. A large quantum computer would break RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curves alike, which is why NIST standardised post-quantum schemes like ML-KEM in 2024.
